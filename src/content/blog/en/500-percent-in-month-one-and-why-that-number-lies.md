The strongest number I can put on a CV comes from Tango Magic. I led the digital transformation of a three-site web ecosystem plus an internal management system, and online went from a side channel to the company's primary revenue channel. Revenue grew more than 500% in the first month after launch.

It is a great number. I also think you should discount it heavily, and I would rather say so before someone asks.

## Why the number is inflated

**The starting base was small.** This is the whole caveat and it is decisive. When the base is low enough, 500% is a smaller absolute change than it sounds, and percentage growth becomes a rhetorical device rather than a measurement. A percentage without its denominator is a claim, not evidence.

**Month one is the launch spike.** New site, announcement, existing customers curious. Some of that was pent-up demand converting at once, not new demand created. The honest question is what month six looked like, and month six is always less flattering than month one.

**It was not only me.** There was a product people already wanted and a company already selling it. I restructured how it was found and bought. That is real, and it is a contribution, not sole authorship.

## What I would defend

Strip the percentage out and the underlying work still stands.

The ecosystem was three sites that did not cohere, plus an internal system that did not talk to them. I restructured it so products were findable, developed and maintained the web assets in JavaScript, PHP, HTML, CSS and WordPress, and integrated REST APIs for inventory, payments and shipping so stock and orders stopped being manual reconciliation. Then analytics and conversion tracking, so the next decision could be made on data instead of opinion, and email marketing pipelines on top.

That is the defensible claim: a company whose online presence was decorative ended up with online as its main channel, and stayed there. The 500% is the headline. The restructure is the work.

## Why volunteer the caveat

Because a number that cannot survive one follow-up question is worse than no number.

If I lead with 500% and someone asks about the base, and I get vague, I have lost the room. Not because the number was bad, but because I now look like someone who does not know the difference between a result and a talking point. Every other claim I make gets re-read in that light.

Saying it first costs almost nothing and buys something specific: whatever I say next gets believed. Calibration is more useful than impressiveness, and it is rarer.

The version I actually use in interviews: *"Revenue grew more than 500% in the first month, and I want to be upfront that the starting base was small. What I would actually point at is the restructure underneath it."*

Nobody has ever been disappointed by that answer. Several people have visibly relaxed.
